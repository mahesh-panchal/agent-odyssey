Gaia’s Rest

  • Reduce hallucinations: Anthropic, docs. Cited by 7 quests; each quest says what to read it for.

    Cited in:

    • Name the Fears: it lists concrete habits such as allowing “I don’t know” and checking claims against quotes, and says plainly that none of them remove errors.
    • Where Machines Fail: it gives habits such as allowing “I don’t know” and checking claims against quotes, the kind of fix for the confident-but-wrong rows in this quest; it says none removes errors.
    • Spot Hallucinations: it lists ways to make a model show its support (quote first, allow “I don’t know”), the opposite of a confident mirage.
    • Ground Truth: it lists model-side habits (quote first, retract unsupported claims); read it to see why a check the model did not write is still needed.
    • Send Small Changes, Say What You Checked: it suggests asking for quotes and allowing “I don’t know”, habits you can request from a collaborator who sends you LLM-assisted work.
    • Same Every Time: it suggests comparing repeated runs, a cheap way to see whether an LLM step gives the same answer each time, and admits such habits reduce but do not remove errors.
    • Boss: Review a Messy Session: it lists ways to ask for support and to allow “I don’t know”, useful when a summary states a flag or default with confidence.

The Codon Mill

  • Token counting: Anthropic, docs. Why read: it documents the free count endpoint and says models from Claude 4.7 onward use a newer tokenizer, so the same text gives roughly 30 percent more tokens than on earlier models (the exact increase depends on the content). Cited in: Tokens, Not Letters.

  • The Strawberry Problem: Cosma et al., EMNLP 2025, paper. Cited by 2 quests; each quest says what to read it for.

    Cited in:

    • Tokens, Not Letters: it argues character-level information is weakly available inside tokens, a leading explanation for miscounted letters.
    • Ground Truth: it studies why models miscount letters and links it to tokens, which is the idea behind miscounted bases.

  • Counting Ability of LLMs and Impact of Tokenization: Zhang et al., arXiv, paper. Cited by 2 quests; each quest says what to read it for.

    Cited in:

    • Tokens, Not Letters: it studies how tokenisation affects counting; its abstract says step-by-step reasoning (chain of thought) can help, and its full text still reports failures with it in modern models. A preprint.
    • Ground Truth: it shows how tokenisation changes counting accuracy in language models, which is why a script, not a model’s reading, should do exact counts (that last step is our general practice, not the paper’s claim). A preprint.

  • CharBench: Evaluating the Role of Tokenization in Character-Level Tasks: arXiv, paper. Why read: a counter-view finding tokenisation only weakly tied to errors, so treat the tokens explanation as likely but disputed. A preprint. Cited in: Tokens, Not Letters.

  • Lost in the Middle: Liu et al., arXiv, paper. Cited by 3 quests; each quest says what to read it for.

    Cited in:

    • Where Machines Fail: it shows recall is best for text at the start or end of a long input and weaker in the middle. A preprint.
    • RAG and Its Cousins: it shows why a long, stuffed prompt can hide the passage that matters.
    • Boss: Review Large Output: it measures how accuracy drops when the relevant fact sits in the middle of a long input.

  • On Faithfulness and Factuality in Abstractive Summarization: Maynez et al., ACL 2020, paper. Cited by 2 quests; each quest says what to read it for.

    Cited in:

    • Where Machines Fail: it found hallucinated content in neural summaries; older models, but it shows why summaries need a spot check.
    • Spot Hallucinations: it found invented content in every neural summary tested; the models are pre-chat-era, so read it for the direction, not current rates.

  • LLM The Genius Paradox: A Linguistic and Math Expert’s Struggle with Simple Word-based Counting Problems: arXiv, paper. Why read: its abstract says specialised math and coding models still struggle with simple counting and that reasoning before answering is the most reliable fix; its full text adds that explicitly asking coding models for Python code worked. A preprint, not peer-reviewed as far as we checked. Cited in: Where Machines Fail.
