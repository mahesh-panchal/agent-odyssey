Choose What to Hand Over

Sage Maelis gathers you in a hall of the Collegium with a warning: left unchecked, an Astral will pull in everything within reach to finish a task, and spread the Blight across the land in the process. As the four travellers imagine what a pact could unlock for their craft, loud boasters brag of giving their Astral free rein over entire projects, dismissing small, guarded tasks as timid half-measures. Maelis points to a wiser way: think of the work as a deep river, and you need not swim the whole span at once. Limit what the Astral sees, step from stone to stone carrying only what the task demands, and check your footing at each stone before you step to the next. She needs a little time to prepare the pacts, so she asks you to return tomorrow and to visit Warden Hesper at the Adventurers’ Training Grounds until then.

  • Goal: you will choose how much of your data and your machine to hand over for one task, and start at the lowest step that gets the job done.
  • Before you start: Know Your Tools. Your own data agreement (the terms under which you received the data) overrides anything here.
  • Time: about 25 minutes. No code or downloads are needed.

The scene

Four people want help from an LLM (a large language model, the kind of tool behind chat assistants). Each pictures what it could do, and then the same two worries arrive: where their data would go, and whether using an LLM means giving a program the run of their computer.

  • Quartermaster Aris
  • Diviner Ilsa
  • Artificer Bob
  • Archivist Elowen

“I keep twelve projects in one spreadsheet. If an LLM could read it, I would have a status report and a risk list for the board by lunch.”

“But where does my data go once I paste it? And a colleague said I would need Claude Code (a coding agent that can read and change files on my computer), which means giving it access to all my files. I cannot allow that, so I will keep writing the report by hand.”

“The sample counts fell in two cohorts. If an LLM could write the query and the first summary, I would have an explanation before the meeting.”

“But the samples database holds participant codes, and my data agreement is strict. Does using an LLM mean installing an agent that can reach the database? Then the answer has to be no LLM at all.”

“Our pipeline has a script nobody has tested. If an LLM could draft the tests and tidy the module, I would clear this week’s backlog by Friday.”

“But an agent that edits files and runs commands in my repository could overwrite something, or run something I never reviewed. It is all or nothing, and nothing feels safer.”

“A collaborator sent a sample sheet with mixed columns and units. If an LLM could clean it and map it to our schema, a week of tidying would take an afternoon.”

“But the sheet holds controlled health metadata. I cannot paste it anywhere, and I do not want an agent loose in the data store. So it is a no.”

All four treat the choice as all or nothing: either no LLM, or an agent with the run of the machine. They have not considered that there is a middle. Between those two ends are several steps, each handing over a little more, and many useful tasks sit on the lower ones. This lesson walks up those steps.

You decide how much to hand over before the first prompt. A wrong choice can send data you meant to keep, or let an agent change files you meant to leave alone.

Two questions

  1. What leaves your machine? A hosted model runs on the provider’s servers, so anything you type, paste or upload is sent there, and so is anything an agent reads. A local model runs on your computer and keeps prompts there, though an agent’s web or tool calls can still reach out.
  2. What may the agent do? Produce text, read files, edit files, or run commands.

Question 1 is about data leaving. Question 2 is about changes on your machine. They vary independently:

  • A chat window with a pasted table: it can only write text, so little can change on your machine, but the whole table is sent.
  • A local model in an agent with access to your files: nothing is sent to a provider, but it can edit files and run commands.

The more data leaves, the higher the first answer. The more the agent may change, the higher the second.

The ladder

A ladder is six ordered steps of how much you hand over. Each step up raises one or both answers. Terms used in the table: a schema is the list of table and column names with no rows; an extract is a small slice of the data; an agent is a model that reads files and runs commands for you; a copy is a duplicate of the data, so the original cannot be edited.

Step What you hand over What leaves
0 Nothing: do the task yourself nothing
1 A question in words, with no data in it your words
2 Structure only: headings, a schema or invented rows. You apply the answer yourself names and invented rows
3 A small extract you are allowed to share, or a local model the extract; nothing if local
4 A read-only agent (plan mode: it reads and proposes, and does not edit) working on a copy everything it reads
5 An agent that edits files and runs commands everything it reads, plus whatever its commands send

Pasting the results of a step 2 task back into the chat sends those rows too, which makes it step 3. Plan mode (the agent reads and proposes, and does not edit) belongs to step 4: a copy protects the original from edits, and anything the agent reads is still sent.

Three questions before any step

  1. What kind of data is it? The table gives a typical starting point. Your agreement and your data steward decide, and they can be stricter. A data steward is the person at your organisation who looks after data rules.
  2. Which account will you use? Consumer and organisation accounts have different terms (next section).
  3. What leaves at this step? Say it in one sentence before you press enter.
Kind of data Typical starting point
Public or published Any step
Internal, not personal Steps 1 to 3; 4 to 5 on an approved account, on a copy
Personal, including pseudonymised codes (codes that replace names) Steps 0 to 2, invented rows only
Health, genetic or otherwise controlled Steps 0 to 2, unless your agreement says otherwise in writing

Under GDPR (the EU data-protection law), pseudonymised codes still count as personal data. Small counts can single people out, so check your policy before pasting results for rare groups.

What a chat window keeps

  • Training: consumer accounts (Free, Pro, Max) have a setting that lets chats improve the models; with it on, data may be kept for up to 5 years. The setting is in your privacy settings.
  • Account type: commercial accounts (Team, Enterprise and the API, which is programmatic access billed per use) are not used for training unless the organisation opts in. The API deletes inputs and outputs within 30 days by default. Team and Enterprise keep saved chats until they are deleted, then remove them within 30 days. Ask your administrator which applies.
  • Deleted chats: they leave the provider’s storage within 30 days.
  • Incognito chats: not used for training, and still kept for 30 days by default. Flagged chats can be kept longer.
  • Screenshots, images and uploads: treat them as data you share, like pasted text.
  • Other providers: their chat tools have their own terms. Read them.

Hands-on: pick the step

For each task, decide three things before you open the answer: the lowest step that does the job, what leaves your machine at that step, and one thing you will check in the result. The tabs hold the same four people, and the tab you chose earlier stays selected.

  • Quartermaster Aris
  • Diviner Ilsa
  • Artificer Bob
  • Archivist Elowen

Task: a one-page status summary and risk list for twelve projects, from a spreadsheet of project names, milestone dates, owner names and status. The data is internal, and owner names are personal data.

Check your answer

The answer is step 2. Paste only the column headings and three invented rows. Ask for an outline of the summary and a formula that counts open milestones per project. Apply both to the real sheet yourself. Only the headings and the invented rows leave. Step 3 would send real rows with owner names, which needs an approved account and your steward’s agreement.

To check: count the open milestones of one project by hand and compare.

Task: explain why the sample count per project fell between two cohorts, from a samples database that holds participant codes under a strict agreement. Pseudonymised codes still count as personal data.

Check your answer

The answer is step 2. Share the schema only and ask for one read-only query. Run it yourself. The table and column names leave, so decide first whether even the names are fine to send, and drop to step 0 if your agreement says they are not. Pasting the results back into the chat would make it step 3.

To check: write a second, simpler count query yourself and compare the two numbers before you explain anything.

Task: write tests for a script that reads sample sheets in your pipeline repository. The code is internal and holds no personal data.

Check your answer

The answer is step 3. Paste the one function (a small allowed extract) and ask for tests. Apply them yourself. Only that function’s code leaves. If you later want an agent to run the tests inside the repository, that is step 5: work on a copy, keep a way to undo, and limit what the agent may do first.

To check: break the function on purpose and confirm that a test fails.

Task: clean and map a sample sheet with controlled health metadata to your schema, under an agreement that forbids sharing the values.

Check your answer

The answer is step 2 at most. Describe the mess in words, give headings and invented rows that copy it, and ask for a validation and mapping script. Run the script locally on the real sheet. Do not ask a model to fill or harmonise the values itself: it can invent plausible ones. Only the headings and the invented rows leave.

To check: compare row counts and a checksum before and after, and keep a log of every change.

Now take your own task. Write the three answers in a sentence each, then ask your data steward the question at the end of the next section if any of them is unclear.

Climbing, if and when you want to

  • Move up a step when the lower step cannot do the task and you can say what leaves.
  • Before steps 4 and 5, work on a copy, keep a way to undo, and limit what the agent may do.
  • For personal or controlled data, a local model (step 3) is allowed only where your agreement says so.
  • If you pasted something you should not have, stop and tell your data steward.
  • Ask your steward: “I want to use [tool, account type] at step [n]. What leaves is [what]. Is that allowed for [data kind]?”
