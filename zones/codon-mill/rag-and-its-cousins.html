RAG and Its Cousins

Travellers keep asking the miller about books that will never fit in the hopper. Some say to fetch a few pages when a question comes, some to draw a web of names, and some to fetch nothing at all. The miller listens to each and asks what is actually being looked for. Before choosing, learn what each way fetches and what it misses.

  • Goal: you will explain what retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is, tell apart its main kinds, and choose between RAG and the other ways of answering questions from a pile of documents.
  • Before you start: The Context Window. This is an optional side quest.
  • Time: about 20 minutes, reading only

Why anyone brings RAG up

This quest helps you pick a method before you build anything, because each method costs time and can fail in a different way. A model knows what it learned in training. It has not read your lab’s protocols, last month’s run logs or the paper that came out last week. You can paste documents into the prompt, but the context window (the text the model can see at once) is finite, and a long prompt costs money and time.

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) is the idea of looking things up first. A program searches your documents for the passages most likely to matter, pastes only those into the prompt, and asks the model to answer from them. The name comes from a 2020 paper that paired a generator with a vector index of Wikipedia. The recipe most people mean today is a later, simpler version of that idea.

Simple RAG in four steps

  1. Chunk. Split the documents into pieces (a chunk is one passage, often a paragraph or a few hundred words).
  2. Embed. An embedding model turns each chunk into a vector (a long list of numbers; texts with similar meaning get vectors that point in similar directions). The vectors are stored in an index.
  3. Retrieve. Embed the question the same way, then fetch the k chunks whose vectors are closest (k is a number you pick, such as 3 or 5).
  4. Generate. Paste those chunks into the prompt and ask the model to answer from them and to say which chunk it used.

Papers and PDFs hold tables, figures and two-column text, and a plain text extractor can scramble them before anything is embedded. Open a few extracted chunks and read them before you trust the index.

Everything the model says is limited by step 3. If the right passage did not come back, the model cannot use it. A good model says it does not know. A weaker one answers from the wrong passages.

A tour of the types

Each type below answers one question: what goes wrong with simple RAG, and what did someone add to fix it?

  • Simple RAG: chunk, embed, top-k, answer. It suits a pile of prose where one or two passages answer each question, and it is cheap to build and update. It struggles with questions that need several passages or the whole collection, since only the top k chunks arrive.
  • Improved RAG: add-ons around the same pipeline. Keyword search (BM25, a classic word-matching score) runs next to embeddings. A reranker (a second model that re-sorts the retrieved chunks) follows. A short context line is added to each chunk before embedding. The question is rewritten, or a made-up answer is drafted first (HyDE) to search with. In one reported test, adding context, BM25 and a reranker cut top-20 retrieval failures from 5.7% to 1.9%. Each add-on is another part to tune, and HyDE’s drafted answer may contain false details.
  • Graph RAG (Microsoft): before any question, an LLM reads every chunk and extracts entities and relationships into a knowledge graph (things as nodes, links between them as edges). It groups the graph into communities and writes a summary of each. It suits questions about the whole collection, such as “what are the main themes?”. Indexing is slow and costly: the paper reports 281 minutes for a roughly 1-million-token corpus with GPT-4 Turbo, and the docs warn it can consume a lot of LLM resources.
  • LightRAG: also builds an entity graph with an LLM, but skips the communities. A query is turned into keywords, matched against entities and relations, and the neighbours are added. New documents merge into the graph without a rebuild. Modes run from plain chunks (naive) to graph-based (local, global, hybrid, mix). It needs a stronger model than plain RAG: its README calls a 30B-parameter model “a reasonable minimum” for local use.
  • Tree RAG (RAPTOR): clusters chunks and summarises them in layers, so a question can match a detail or a summary. It suits long documents where the answer may be a broad theme, and it adds another LLM build step.
  • Loop-style RAG (Self-RAG, Corrective RAG, agentic RAG): the system decides when to retrieve, checks what came back, tries again or searches the web. It suits questions where one lookup is rarely enough, at the price of more model calls per question. Self-RAG needs a model trained for it.

Does the graph pay off?

The papers that introduced Graph RAG and LightRAG report wins over plain RAG, mostly on whole-collection questions, as judged by an LLM. Check any such result on your own questions. Independent studies give a mixed picture:

  • No consistent winner. A 2026 comparison found complementary behaviours. On single-fact questions from Natural Questions, plain RAG scored best (F1 64.78 against 63.01 for the best graph variant). On multi-hop questions (an answer that needs facts from two or more passages) a graph method led by a few points.
  • Graphs cost more to build. 135 seconds for plain RAG against 5,560 for one Graph RAG variant on the same corpus. Only about 65% of the answer entities ended up in the graphs.
  • LLM judges are order-sensitive. A judge can flip its verdict when you swap the order of the two answers, so win-rate numbers are weak evidence.
  • Task decides. Graphs help when a task connects ideas across documents, and can add noise on simple lookups.

Start with simple RAG, measure it on questions you care about, and add a graph only if the failures are of the kind a graph addresses (multi-hop or whole-collection questions) and you can afford the extra cost.

When RAG is the right tool

RAG is one of several ways to get a model to work from your documents. Choose by your situation:

Your situation Often a better fit
Whole collection under about 200,000 tokens (roughly 500 pages) Put it all in the prompt
Same large documents asked about again and again Long context with caching
Files you can search by name or exact text Agent search tools such as glob and grep
Tables and databases (counts, filters, joins) A query tool: SQL, an API or an MCP server
Exact words matter (gene names, sample ids) Keyword search first, or hybrid
You want a format or style Better prompts, a Skill, or fine-tuning
Large, changing prose collection, answers in passages RAG
  • Small collection: below about 200,000 tokens you can include everything with no need for RAG. Prompt caching (reusing an unchanged prompt prefix at a lower price) makes repeated questions cheaper. Check the current pricing and cache lifetime.
  • Repeated questions: cache-augmented generation (preload everything once) was faster than RAG on small question-answering sets, and becomes impractical for significantly larger datasets.
  • Searchable files: an agent can navigate code and configs “just in time” with glob and grep. Exploring at run time is slower than reading a pre-built index.
  • Tables and databases: a vector index returns the nearest passages, so it usually cannot promise a complete filter or an exact count. A query can, and you can read the query.
  • Exact words: BM25 is a strong baseline in the BEIR benchmark, and adding it to embeddings cut retrieval failures further.
  • Format or style: fine-tune when the problem is consistency or behaviour, and supply context when the model lacks knowledge.
  • RAG: the collection does not fit in the window, and you can update the index without retraining anything.

In one study with 2024 models, a long-context model beat RAG on average given enough budget, while RAG cost much less and gave the same answer on over 60% of queries. Its “Self-Route” idea tries RAG first and falls back to the long prompt, which cut cost by 39 to 65% on two models.

What can go wrong with RAG itself

  • The right passage never comes back. Retrieval misses are normal. Measure two things separately: did the right chunk appear, and did the answer follow from the chunks it was given? The second is called faithfulness.
  • Too much arrives. More chunks can distract the model, and models use text in the middle of a long prompt less well than text at the start or end.
  • Retrieved text can carry instructions. A document someone else wrote may contain lines aimed at your model, and RAG does not fully remove this risk. Limit what an agent may do after reading retrieved text.
  • Chunks leave your machine. With a hosted model, retrieved passages are sent to the provider as ordinary prompt text. Your data agreement decides whether that is allowed.
  • The index goes stale. Anything you indexed last month does not know about today’s edits until you rebuild it.

Lore

Further reading. Optional.

  • Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks: Lewis and colleagues, arXiv, paper. Why read: it is the 2020 paper that named RAG, so you can see how much the common recipe has been simplified since.
  • Introducing Contextual Retrieval: Anthropic, article. Why read: it walks through the improved-RAG add-ons with reported numbers and states the 200,000-token rule of thumb for skipping RAG.
  • From Local to Global: A Graph RAG Approach to Query-Focused Summarization: Edge and colleagues (Microsoft), paper. Why read: it explains the entity graph, communities and summaries, and reports the indexing cost.
  • LightRAG: Simple and Fast Retrieval-Augmented Generation: Guo and colleagues, paper. Why read: it shows how keyword-based retrieval over an entity graph differs from community summaries.
  • RAG vs. GraphRAG: A Systematic Evaluation and Key Insights: Han and colleagues, paper. Why read: it is an independent comparison with costs and a warning about order effects in LLM judges.
  • Retrieval Augmented Generation or Long-Context LLMs?: Li and colleagues (Google), paper. Why read: it compares RAG with putting everything in the prompt and proposes a hybrid.
  • Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts: Liu and colleagues, paper. Why read: it shows why a long, stuffed prompt can hide the passage that matters.
  • Effective context engineering for AI agents: Anthropic Engineering, article. Why read: it describes loading information just in time with search tools instead of pre-building an index.
