RAG and Its Cousins

Prerequisites: The Context Window. This is an optional side quest. Objective: explain what retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is, tell apart simple RAG, improved RAG, Graph RAG, LightRAG and the loop-style variants, and choose between RAG and the other ways of answering questions from a pile of documents. Time: about 20 minutes. Reading only; the hands-on follow-up is Grow a Graph with Ollama.

Last verified (docs, not run): the papers, docs and posts listed in Lore, read on 2026-11-20. Sources: research/rag.md. Numbers are the authors’ own unless the text says otherwise.

Why anyone brings RAG up

A model knows what it learned in training. It has not read your lab’s protocols, last month’s run logs or the paper that came out last week. You can paste documents into the prompt, but the window (the text the model can see at once; see The Context Window) is finite, and a long prompt costs money and time.

RAG is the idea of looking things up first. A program searches your documents for the passages most likely to matter, pastes only those into the prompt, and asks the model to answer from them. The name comes from a 2020 paper by Lewis and colleagues, which paired a generator with a vector index of Wikipedia. The recipe most people mean today is a later, simpler version of that idea.

Simple RAG in four steps

  1. Chunk. Split the documents into pieces (a chunk is one passage, often a paragraph or a few hundred words).
  2. Embed. An embedding model turns each chunk into a vector (a long list of numbers; texts with similar meaning get vectors that point in similar directions). The vectors are stored in an index.
  3. Retrieve. Embed the question the same way, then fetch the k chunks whose vectors are closest (k is a number you pick, such as 3 or 5).
  4. Generate. Paste those chunks into the prompt and ask the model to answer from them and to say which chunk it used.

Step 1 has its own pitfalls. Papers and PDFs hold tables, figures and two-column text, and a plain text extractor can scramble them before anything is embedded. A community notebook collection describes a results table losing its structure this way (we did not run it). General practice: open a few extracted chunks and read them before you trust the index.

Everything the model says is limited by step 3. If the right passage did not come back, the model cannot use it, and a good model will either say it does not know or, worse, answer from the wrong passages.

A tour of the types

Names overlap and new ones appear every month. Think of them as answers to the question: what goes wrong with simple RAG, and what did someone add to fix it?

Type What it adds Good for Watch out for
Simple RAG Chunk, embed, top-k, answer. A pile of prose where each question is answered by one or two passages. Cheap to build and to update. Questions that need several passages, or the whole collection, since only the top k chunks arrive.
Improved RAG Add-ons around the same pipeline: keyword search (BM25, a classic word-matching score) next to embeddings, a reranker (a second model that re-sorts the retrieved chunks), a short context line added to each chunk before embedding, and rewriting the question or drafting a made-up answer first (HyDE) to search with. Raising the chance the right chunk comes back. Anthropic reports that adding context, BM25 and a reranker cut its top-20 retrieval failures from 5.7% to 1.9% on its own tests. Each add-on is another moving part to tune. HyDE’s own paper says the drafted answer “is unreal and may contain false details”.
Graph RAG (Microsoft) Before any question, an LLM reads every chunk and extracts entities and relationships into a knowledge graph (things as nodes, links between them as edges). It groups the graph into communities and writes a summary of each. Questions about the whole collection, such as “what are the main themes?”, answered from community summaries. Indexing is slow and costly: the paper reports 281 minutes for a roughly 1-million-token corpus with GPT-4 Turbo. The docs warn that it “can consume a lot of LLM resources”.
LightRAG Also builds an entity graph with an LLM, but skips the communities. A query is turned into keywords, matched against entities and relations, and the neighbours are added. New documents merge into the graph without a rebuild. A lighter graph approach that updates incrementally, with modes from plain chunks (naive) to graph-based (local, global, hybrid, mix). The project says it needs a stronger model than plain RAG: its README calls a 30B-parameter model “a reasonable minimum” for local use.
Tree RAG (RAPTOR) Clusters chunks and summarises them in layers, so a question can match a detail or a summary. Long documents where the answer may be a broad theme. Another build step with an LLM; tested mainly on question-answering benchmarks.
Loop-style RAG (Self-RAG, Corrective RAG, agentic RAG) The system decides when to retrieve, checks what came back, tries again or searches the web. A survey groups these as “agentic RAG”. Questions where one lookup is rarely enough. More model calls per question. Self-RAG needs a model trained for it.

General practice: the “good for” column is our reading of what each method is built to do. The “watch out” figures come from the sources named in Lore.

Does the graph pay off?

The papers that introduced Graph RAG and LightRAG report wins over plain RAG. Those results are self-reported: an LLM judge compared answers on questions that another LLM generated, mostly about whole collections, and the authors of LightRAG note that ground truth is hard to define for such questions.

Independent studies give a more mixed picture:

  • A 2026 comparison (Han and colleagues) found “complementary behaviors rather than a consistent winner”. On single-fact questions from Natural Questions, plain RAG scored best (F1 64.78 against 63.01 for the best graph variant here). On multi-hop questions (an answer that needs facts from two or more passages) a graph method led by a few points. Building the graphs took far longer (135 seconds for plain RAG against 5,560 for one Graph RAG variant on the same corpus), and only about 65% of the answer entities ended up in the graphs.
  • The same study found that an LLM judge can flip its verdict when you swap the order of the two answers. Treat win-rate numbers as weak evidence.
  • A second study (Xiang and colleagues) concludes that graphs help when a task connects ideas across documents and can add noise on simple lookups.

General practice: start with simple RAG, measure it on questions you care about, and add a graph only if the failures are of the kind a graph addresses (multi-hop or whole-collection questions) and you can afford the extra cost.

When RAG is the right tool

RAG is one of several ways to get a model to work from your documents. Here is how to choose.

Your situation Often a better fit Why
The whole collection is small (under about 200,000 tokens, roughly 500 pages) Put it all in the prompt. Anthropic’s post says that below that size you can include everything with no need for RAG. Prompt caching (reusing an unchanged prompt prefix at a lower price) makes repeated questions cheaper. Check the current pricing and cache lifetime.
The same large documents are asked about again and again Long context with caching, or cache-augmented generation (preload everything once). One paper found it faster than RAG on small question-answering sets, and its authors say it “becomes impractical for significantly larger datasets”.
Files you can search by name or exact text (code, configs, scripts) Let an agent use search tools such as glob and grep. Anthropic describes Claude Code navigating “just-in-time” with these, and says exploring at run time is slower than reading a pre-built index.
Tables and databases (“all samples deeper than 30x in liver tissue”, counts, joins) A query tool: SQL, an API or an MCP server. General practice (no source opened): a vector index returns the nearest passages, so it cannot promise a complete filter or an exact count. A query can, and you can read the query.
Exact words matter (gene names, sample ids, error codes) Keyword search first, or hybrid. BM25 is a strong baseline in the BEIR benchmark, and adding it to embeddings cut Anthropic’s retrieval failures further.
You want the model to follow a format or style Better prompts, a Skill, or fine-tuning. OpenAI’s guide says to fine-tune when the problem is consistency or behaviour, and to supply context when the model lacks knowledge. Two papers found fine-tuning a poor way to add new facts. They did not test LoRA, so that is an inference.
A large, changing collection of prose, and questions answered by particular passages RAG. This is the case it was made for: the collection does not fit in the window, and you can update the index without retraining anything.

A study from Google researchers found that, with enough budget, a long-context model beat RAG on average, while RAG cost much less and gave the same answer on over 60% of queries. Their “Self-Route” idea tries RAG first and falls back to the long prompt, which cut cost by 39 to 65% on two models. These were 2024 models.

What can go wrong with RAG itself

  • The right passage never comes back. Retrieval misses are normal. Measure them separately from the model’s answer: did the right chunk appear? and was the answer supported by the chunks it was given? The Ragas paper defines the second one as faithfulness. Splitting the two checks is our suggestion.
  • Too much arrives. More chunks can distract the model, and models use text in the middle of a long prompt less well than text at the start or end (see The Context Window and the “Lost in the Middle” paper in Lore).
  • Retrieved text can carry instructions. A document someone else wrote may contain lines aimed at your model. OWASP says RAG does not fully remove this risk. See Guard the Door for habits that limit the damage.
  • Chunks leave your machine. With a hosted model, retrieved passages are sent to the provider as ordinary prompt text. Whether that is allowed for your data is decided by your agreement; see The Locked Cabinet.
  • The index goes stale. Anything you indexed last month does not know about today’s edits until you rebuild it. General practice.

Lore

Further reading. Optional.

  • Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks: Lewis and colleagues, arXiv, paper. Why read: it is the 2020 paper that named RAG, so you can see how much the common recipe has been simplified since.
  • Introducing Contextual Retrieval: Anthropic, article. Why read: it walks through the improved-RAG add-ons with reported numbers and states the 200,000-token rule of thumb for skipping RAG.
  • From Local to Global: A Graph RAG Approach to Query-Focused Summarization: Edge and colleagues (Microsoft), paper. Why read: it explains the entity graph, communities and summaries, and reports the indexing cost.
  • LightRAG: Simple and Fast Retrieval-Augmented Generation: Guo and colleagues, paper. Why read: it shows how keyword-based retrieval over an entity graph differs from community summaries.
  • RAG vs. GraphRAG: A Systematic Evaluation and Key Insights: Han and colleagues, paper. Why read: it is an independent comparison with costs and a warning about order effects in LLM judges.
  • Retrieval Augmented Generation or Long-Context LLMs?: Li and colleagues (Google), paper. Why read: it compares RAG with putting everything in the prompt and proposes a hybrid.
  • Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts: Liu and colleagues, paper. Why read: it shows why a long, stuffed prompt can hide the passage that matters.
  • Effective context engineering for AI agents: Anthropic Engineering, article. Why read: it describes loading information just in time with search tools instead of pre-building an index.
