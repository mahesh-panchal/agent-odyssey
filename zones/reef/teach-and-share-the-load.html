Teach and Share the Load

Prerequisites: The Reviewer’s Code. Objective: pass on what you learned without leaving anyone behind, and keep your own skills and workload healthy. Time: about 15 minutes. General practice, not a vendor claim. Team norms you agree locally override these defaults.

Teaching

You can help without being an expert. When teaching a colleague:

  • Start from their problem; the tool comes later.
  • Teach the checks first. Verification habits matter more than clever prompts.
  • Let them predict, then reveal. Ask “what do you expect this to do?” before running it.
  • Welcome opt-outs. A colleague may choose not to use LLMs, and may still be an excellent reviewer.

Protecting your own learning

An agent can quietly take over the parts you wanted to learn. Pick a rule that suits you, such as: write the first version of anything new yourself, then compare; ask the agent to explain while you do the work; and keep one area of your work agent-free.

Owning what you made

Finishing a project mostly through an agent can leave a quiet feeling that the result is not really yours, a kind of imposter syndrome. Many people report it, and it points to something useful: ownership grows from your decisions and your understanding. These habits build both:

  • Make the decisions. Choose the question, the method and what counts as good enough. Let the agent draft.
  • Explain it back. Before you call the work done, walk a colleague through each step.
  • Keep a short decision log. Note what you chose, what you changed, what you set aside, and why.
  • Do one hard part yourself. Pick the part you most want to understand.
  • Credit the agent’s part plainly in your methods or commit messages. Honest credit lifts the pressure.
  • Judge the result by what you can explain and defend.

General practice. Nothing here depends on a tool or a version.

Managing workload

Agents make it easy to start many things. Count the cost of reviewing what they produce. A simple rule: start only as much as you can review today.

Keeping timelines honest

A quick draft can quietly raise what everyone expects. A first version that once took a day now arrives in an hour, and the time for reading, checking and understanding it stays what it always was. These habits help keep the pace realistic:

  • Say where the time goes. Drafting is fast. Reading the diff, running the checks and making sense of the result take the time they take.
  • Agree timelines before you start, and put review time in the estimate.
  • Share the whole picture with the people asking: how long the draft took and how long checking took.
  • Hand over a quick draft as a draft, and say so.
  • Spend the time you saved on checking.

Keeping your voice

Heavy use of an agent can smooth your writing into a general style. These habits keep it yours:

  • Draft the first version in your own words, then edit.
  • Read it aloud. Your ear spots phrases you would never say.
  • Keep a sample of your earlier writing as a reference for how you sound.
  • Use the agent for structure and consistency checks, such as headings, terms and abbreviations.
  • The same goes for emails to collaborators, and for your voice in letters and reviews.

Keeping the creative part

Checking drafts can crowd out the thinking you enjoy. These habits protect it:

  • Keep the question, the design and the interpretation for yourself.
  • Give the agent the routine drafting, such as boilerplate, formatting and first-pass summaries.
  • Schedule checking as real work, with time in your calendar.

Trusting your own judgement

A ready second opinion can wear down confidence in your own call. Keep it in practice:

  • Decide first and write your call down, then ask for a second opinion.
  • Keep some decisions agent-free.
  • Compare afterwards. Note where your call held up and where the agent saw something you missed.
