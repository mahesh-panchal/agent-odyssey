Brannoch the tinker lost his workshop, Sefa the scholar published a tree built on phantom citations, Tam the steward pasted restricted records into a chat, and Oleander the clerk shipped a demo that “ran fine”. Each was warned by the academics, and each was sure it would not happen to them. Every worry in the room has a habit that answers it. Name yours here, then go and meet the creatures.

  • Goal: you will name what worries you about large language model (LLM) agents and pick the habit that answers each worry, whether you plan to use them or not.

  • Time: about 10 minutes.

Where people start

Before you learn any habit, it helps to name the worry it answers. People come to these tools from different places:

  • Some are keen and want to know how to begin well.
  • Some are curious and want to see what is involved before deciding.
  • Some hold back, often for good reasons: the environmental cost of AI, harm to people and their work, or the quality of what comes out.

Every one of these starting points is reasonable, and nothing here asks you to use these tools. This course leaves the larger questions to you. It covers the practical worries that come with using an agent and gives each one a habit that makes the risk smaller. Whether you go on to use agents or decide to leave them alone, you will have a clear picture of what they ask of you.

An agent is a language model running inside a program that picks and runs tools (such as reading a file or running a command) in a loop toward a goal. A prompt is the text you send to the model. The rest of the course trains the habits below, and each row links to the lesson that teaches it.

About the output

The worry The habit Where you learn it
“It makes things up.” Check against something the agent did not write. Sequence Maze
“It produces slop in bulk.” Take small steps, test before code, keep a review budget. Sequence Maze: Wall of Output
“I do not understand what it did.” Ask for a changelog and explain it back. Sequence Maze: Keep a Paper Trail

Slop is low-quality bulk output. A review budget means you only produce as much as you can honestly review in a day.

About the work and your data

The worry The habit Where you learn it
“It could delete or overwrite my work.” Undo with git, set permission rules, work on a scratch copy. Ancient DNA Crypt
“It could leak my data or my secrets.” Keep secrets and private data out of prompts; use an approved account or a local model. Ancient DNA Crypt
“Someone could see my unpublished results and publish first.” Read your tool’s data terms; keep unpublished work out of consumer chat tools. Ancient DNA Crypt

A sandbox is a layer of the operating system that limits what commands can touch; it is one way to protect your files. A scratch copy is a duplicate you let the agent work on, so the original stays safe. For unpublished work, prefer an agreement your institution has reviewed or a local model, and record the date you made each result.

About you and the people around you

The worry The habit Where you learn it
“It will stop me learning.” Write it first, predict before you look, ask it to explain. Biodiversity Reef
“Someone else has to review all this.” Review it yourself first, say you used an LLM, send small changes. Biodiversity Reef
“It will feel hollow, as if it was not my work.” Make the decisions, explain each step, keep a decision log. Biodiversity Reef
“Everyone will expect work faster now.” Say where the time goes; agree timelines that include review. Biodiversity Reef
“Everything I write will sound the same.” Draft in your own words first, then edit. Biodiversity Reef
“I will spend my days checking drafts.” Keep the question and interpretation; count checking as work. Biodiversity Reef
“People will think I am lazy or unoriginal.” Say what the agent did and what you checked. Biodiversity Reef
“I will stop trusting my own judgement.” Decide first, then ask for a second opinion. Biodiversity Reef

For voice and judgement, use the agent for structure and checks, and keep a few decisions agent-free. For credit and workload, follow your team’s norm, credit the agent’s part openly and share how long checking took.

Lore

Further reading. Optional.

  • Reduce hallucinations: Anthropic, docs. Why read: it lists concrete habits such as allowing “I don’t know” and checking claims against quotes, and says plainly that none of them remove errors.
  • Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models: Sharma et al. (Anthropic authors), ICLR 2024, paper. Why read: it shows assistants often agree with a user’s stated view, so you can see why asking for the case against your idea helps.
  • Mitigating prompt injections in browser use: Anthropic, article. Why read: a vendor’s own account that injection is not solved and what defences exist; the attack figures are Anthropic’s own tests.
  • Security: Anthropic, docs. Why read: it describes the built-in protections and advice on untrusted content, a practical start for the fear of prompt injection.
